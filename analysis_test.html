1. Introduction

This report outlines the full data analysis pipeline for analyzing how respondents perceive and rate their preference for various genres. The core dependent variable asks respondents to rate genres on a 1-to-7 ordinal scale:

  • 1 = Strongly Dislike
  • 4 = Neither Like Nor Dislike
  • 7 = Strongly Like

The primary analytical goal is to evaluate how sociodemographic factors, cultural capital (Education, Parental Education, Childhood Arts), and ideological leanings (e.g., Social and Economic Conservatism) influence these tastes across the rating scale.

2. Data Preparation and Reshaping

The data pipeline begins with raw survey data.

  1. Recoding and Cleaning:
    • Demographic variables (age, education, income, gender, race) are cleaned and transformed.
    • Continuous predictors like Social Conservatism (social_c), Economic Conservatism (economic_c), Education (educ_c), Parental Education (peduc_c), Childhood Arts (arts_c), Income (income_c), and Age (age_c) are mean-centered and scaled. Centering ensures that the model intercepts represent the “average” survey respondent, aiding in convergence and interpretation.
  2. Long-Format Reshaping:
    • To leverage multilevel modeling, the separate genre rating columns are pivoted into a “long” dataset. Each row represents a single respondent’s rating of a single genre.
    • This structure allows the model to treat both respondent_id and genre as grouping variables (random effects), controlling for individual response tendencies and baseline differences between genres.

3. Modeling Strategy: Bayesian Adjacent Category (ACAT) Models

Because the outcome is a 1-to-7 ordinal scale, standard linear regression is inappropriate (it assumes the conceptual “distance” between a 1 and 2 is identical to a 6 and 7). Instead, we utilize Bayesian Multilevel Ordinal Models via the brms package.

We selected the Adjacent Category (ACAT) family. Unlike cumulative ordinal models, ACAT models predict the probability of choosing category \(k+1\) rather than category \(k\). This aligns conceptually with how survey respondents make step-by-step decisions on a Likert scale.

Structural Model Hierarchy

We systematically fitted a hierarchy of competing model specifications to the data:

  1. Baseline Strict (Model 1): Assumes that continuous predictors exert a uniform, linear push across all thresholds of the scale, identical for all genres.
  2. Relaxed Category-Specific (CS) (Model 2): Relaxes the proportional odds assumption using the cs() specification. This allows the slope of predictors to vary across each step of the rating scale, capturing non-linear “centrist” or “polarizing” effects.
  3. Random Slopes (RS) Strict (Model 3): Allows the effect of demographic predictors to vary independently for every genre.
  4. Variance Strict (Distributional) (Model 4): Introduces a second equation predicting the disc parameter (inverse variance/consensus) using demographics, testing if groups differ in how much they agree on genre ratings.
  5. Variance + Random Slopes (Model 5): The ultimate hybrid, applying genre-specific random slopes to both the location and the variance equations.
  6. Relaxed CS + Random Slopes (Model 6): Relaxes slopes for specific categories and genres simultaneously.

4. Model Comparison

To determine which mathematical architecture is empirically justified, we compared predictive performance using the Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC) subsetted to 1,000 random posterior draws.

7. Cultural Consensus and Variance (Distributional Model)

To answer the question of whether there is widespread public agreement (consensus) vs. polarization (high variance) on these genre ratings, we look at the results of the Distributional (Location-Scale) ACAT Model.

7.1 Baseline Variance by Genre

By extracting both the location intercept and the disc parameter intercept, we can map the entire landscape in two dimensions: baseline likability (Location) and how much the public agrees on it (Consensus).

  • Higher disc parameters mean higher consensus (lower variance).
  • Lower disc parameters (negative) mean lower consensus (more chaotic, polarized, or spread-out ratings).

9. Conclusion

By systematically scaling up from baseline strict location models to fully distributional location-scale models with random slopes, we successfully map the social structure of taste.

  1. Model fit comparisons overwhelmingly mandate the use of Variance (Distributional) components combined with Random Slopes.
  2. Sociodemographic characteristics not only shift which genres respondents prefer, but critically alter the variance and consensus of those preferences across the public landscape.
